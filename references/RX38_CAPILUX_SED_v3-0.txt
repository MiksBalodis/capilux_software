ABSTRACT
The experimental project CAPILUX, developed by students at Riga Technical
University specifically for the REXUS project. The main task of the CAPILUX
experiment is to test four chambers with different shapes and different surfaces
for moving fluid in a given direction. The CAPILUX experiment utilizes four
chambers in total. Three chambers feature the distinct developed geometries,
while the fourth one has no geometry and serves as a control chamber. During
the flight, the experiment will be recorded by cameras. Each individual chamber records the progress of the experiment and saves the data onto separate
SD cards. This data will be used to establish correlations between the specific
shapes and surfaces to conduct the macro analysis of the flow in the near microgravity conditions.
The CAPILUX project plan carries a potential contribution to the space industry
by supporting the creation of new types of liquid storage systems for space
vehicles.

1 INTRODUCTION
Fluid management is a major challenge in space systems due to a lack of
gravity-driven forces that govern fluid behavior on Earth. In microgravity environments, conventional fluid management techniques like buoyancy and hydrostatic pressure gradients are negligible, leading to unpredictable fluid distribution and phase separation. This creates significant difficulties for spacecraft
subsystems, including propellant storage, thermal regulation, and life support
systems, all of which rely on reliable fluid transport and positioning.
To address these challenges, capillary-driven fluid management has emerged
as a promising alternative. By exploiting surface tension and wetting properties, fluids can be guided and controlled through carefully designed geometries
and surface treatments. However, the relationship between container geometry,
surface characteristics, and fluid behavior in microgravity remains insufficiently
understood, particularly under dynamic conditions.
The CAPILUX experiment was designed to investigate fluid behavior in specially
designed structures of varying geometries and surface properties. The experiment aims to analyze fluid movement and behavior in structured chambers and
a control chamber to develop guidelines for designing an efficient system of
passive fluid transport in microgravity environments.
Throughout this SED, the REXUS User Manual has been referenced for guidance and requirements. [1]
1.1 Scientific/Technical Background
In the absence of hydrostatic pressure, fluid behavior is determined by surface
tension and wetting effects rather than gravity in microgravity. This phenomenon
alters fluid transport and phase separation compared to terrestrial conditions
and poses significant challenges for space systems such as propellant management devices (PMDs), thermal control loops, life support systems, and microfluidic payloads. [2][3]
Surface tension-based PMDs utilize capillary rise and internal corner flow to control fluid position and provide gas-free propellant delivery in microgravity. Such
devices, including vane, sponge, and mesh channel PMDs, have a rich history of
aeronautical applications and have been implemented in spacecraft propulsion
systems, beginning with early missions such as the Viking orbiter and continuing
into applications for storable propellant satellites.
Surface wettability is a critical parameter in capillary systems, as the contact
angle directly affects capillary pressure and flow stability. Therefore, PMDs rely
on materials and surface treatments that ensure reliable wetting by the working
fluid or propellant. In practice, this is achieved primarily through the selection
of materials and compatible surface coatings. Although wettability-enhancing
coatings have been described in patents and technical documentation related
to PMDs, they are rarely encountered in flight-ready systems. They are an active
area of research in fluid systems in reduced gravity environments and offer the
potential for advanced passive flow control. [4]

1.2 Mission Statement
Reliable passive fluid management in microgravity is a critical challenge for
spacecraft fluid storage systems. Capillary-based solutions are widely used;
however, existing design models rely heavily on ground tests, simplified geometries, or short-term microgravity experiments. This leaves significant uncertainty
in predicting fluid behaviour in realistic 3D structures. The combined effects of
geometry, surface wettability, and residual accelerations on capillary rise and
flow stability are insufficiently supported by flight data.
The CAPILUX mission is to experimentally investigate passive, pumpless fluid
transport and storage in near-microgravity conditions and provide flight-validated
data for improvement of capillary systems. The experiment will allow a qualitative and quantitative assessment of how design parameters influence fluid behaviour by comparing capillary rise dynamics in several sealed chambers with
different geometries and surface characteristics under controlled rotation and
unwinding. The resulting dataset will enable the development of robust competencies for the design of passive fluid control devices, which will directly contribute to the development of more reliable fluid systems for spacecraft.
1.3 Experiment Objectives
The objective of the CAPILUX experiment is to experimentally investigate passive, pumpless fluid transport in microgravity and obtain flight-validated data
RX38_CAPILUX_SED_v3-0_26Aug26

- 22 -
for the design and validation of capillary-based fluid management systems for
space applications. The experiment aims to understand how geometry and surface properties influence capillary rise and meniscus dynamics in sealed fluid
systems.
Primary Experiment Goals:
• To compare capillary flow behaviour in chambers with different geometries
and internal structures, identifying qualitative differences in fluid distribution
and flow stability.
Secondary Experiment Objectives:
• To investigate the role of surface wettability and material properties in capillary
rise and meniscus stability in passive fluid systems.
• To compare experimental observations with numerical models to identify model
limitations and improve prediction accuracy for complex 3D geometries.
• To create a publicly available dataset of synchronized images and sensor measurements for use by the broader scientific and educational community.
• To investigate fluid distribution in a spin axis offset test chamber, observer the
fluid distribution during the de-spin and microgravity manouver
The experiment is limited to passive, pumpless fluid transfer in sealed chambers and does not consider active flow control, fluid behaviour long-term after
the microgravity phase, or propellant-specific chemical effects. The results are
intended for qualitative validation and comparative analysis, not for full-scale
flight hardware qualification.

By providing individual monitoring for each chamber, each containing a unique
capillary channel geometry, the integrity and purity of the experimental data are
maintained. The optical configuration of the system is designed to enable precise quantitative analysis of the fluid travel distance and verification of the contact angle based on the acquired video. Each chamber in the experiment is
planned to contain 15 ml (with ± 1 ml margin of error) of liquid for fluid transport evaluation. This volume is proportional to the chamber capacity, ensuring
high-quality visual observation of the experiment. While the rocket is rolling the
liquid would migrate to the outer ends of the radially placed chambers, once despin took place the liquid would not be accelerated and would be free to travel
along the structures to the other end of the chamber ow. Since the objective of
the project is to test the capillary effect, the data processing components must
remain simple. Therefore, standard distilled water was chosen as the experimental fluid. The fluid will be coloured to improve visibility.
Design of Test Chambers Based on existing research for chamber geometries,
several variations were selected. These are derived from previously flight-proven
shapes as well as capillary interactions found in nature.
Chamber 1 (V-Shape) shape is based on equation 1.4 for open channel wedges.
CFD analysis is performed to determine best single wedge shape.
RX38_CAPILUX_SED_v3-0_26Aug26

- 24 -
Figure 1.6. – V-shape structure.
Chamber 2 (Tree like) shape is rooted in the most efficient natural use of capillary
action: trees. The superiority of tree-like structures for fluid transport is wellproven. By applying these biological principles, the second chamber geometry
was developed. The plan is to add pores to the structure to increase capillary
action.
Figure 1.7. – Tree like shape structure.
Chamber 3 (Valve like) design is based on existing study of the two-dimensional
directional fin type structures. The design was adapted to the three-dimensional
structure and was repeated multiple times and arranged in a honeycomb pattern
throughout the chamber. [9]

3.3 Interdependencies
CAPILUX project consists of multiple interconnected work packages, where the
progress of one task directly affects subsequent activities. Scientific research,
CFD simulations, mechanical design, electronics, software, manufacturing, and
verification activities are closely linked throughout the project development process.
One of the most critical interdependencies within the project is the acquisition of
the camera systems and the successful vacuum chamber testing of the experiment test chambers. Since the experiment relies heavily on synchronized visual
observation and sealed chamber validation, delays in camera procurement or
unsuccessful vacuum testing may directly affect integration, verification, and
flight readiness activities.
The chamber geometry and fluid selection must be finalized before CFD analysis and mechanical manufacturing can begin. Electronics and software development depend on finalized system interfaces and sensor integration. System
integration and verification activities, including leak, thermal, and vacuum tests,
can only be performed after successful subsystem assembly. Delays in procurement, manufacturing, or external testing availability may affect the overall
project schedule; therefore, early prototyping and iterative testing are used to
reduce project risks and ensure system readiness before launch.
During the summer we had less time to work on the project and because the fact
that most of the university was closed for summer it was difficult to get access
to the vacuum camera. We plan to restart working on this project much more
diligently once the study period begins and conduct more vacuum camera tests.

4.5 CFD Design
4.5.1 Contact angle measurement
Contact angle value between surface and liquid play major role in CAPILUX
experiment so different surface and liquid interactions must be quantified which
is done by utilizing the contact angle, which is measured by using the contact
angle sessile drop method. Contact angle value is a requirement by the CFD
software to more accurately simulate the capillary flow.
4.5.2 Surface coating selection
Surface coating is selected based on three criteria:
1. Contact angle measurement
2. Coating applicability
3. Coating durability
Two types of surfaces are required for the success of the experiment- hydrophilic
and hydrophobic surfaces. Hydrophobicity will be utilized to ensure that the fluid
doesn’t stick where it shouldn’t during the experiment, hydrophilicity will help
increase the capillarity.
Each coating contact value is quantified by measuring 6 different droplets on
top of it on 3 different places on surfaces, then the average value is used as the
contact angle value.
Figure 4.14. – Contact angle measurement example.
Hydrophobic surface coating
The test chamber walls (acrylic test tube) and end cap faces which are inside the
test chamber must be hydrophobic. To ensure this potential surface coatings are
investigated. The surfaces and surface coatings investigated for hydrophobic
properties are:
1. Acrylic without any coatings
RX38_CAPILUX_SED_v3-0_26Aug26

- 73 -
2. Acrylic coated with “wood wax”
3. Acrylic coated with epoxy
Figure 4.15. – Hydrophobic coating contact angle comparison.
Based on Figure 4.15, acrylic tube will be coated with small “wood wax” layer
in such a way that the transparency is still maintained. From experimentation it
was observed that small layer of wax doesn’t interfere with the acrylic’s visibility
Hydrophilic surface coating
The PMD is additively manufactured using 3D printing technology, because the
contact angles are relatively high for untreated 3D printed plastic surface, surface coatings which would reduce the contact angles as much as possible are
investigated. The surfaces and surface coatings investigated for hydrophilic
properties are:
1. PETG FDM untreated
2. Photopolymer resin SLA untreated
3. PVA (11%) + PVP (10%) + water (79%) coating
4. PVA (11%) + PVP (10%) + water (79%) + borax (grains) coating
5. PVA (11%) + PVP (10%) + water (79%) + borax (mixture) coating
6. PETG PVD (Physical Vapor Deposition) steel coating
7. PVA D3 coating
RX38_CAPILUX_SED_v3-0_26Aug26

- 74 -
Figure 4.16. – Hydrophilic coating contact angle comparison.
Based on Figure 4.16 PVA based coatings are the best candidates for applicability. We believe that PVA + PVP + water + borax (mix) would be the best surface
coating solution for our application when considering both the contact angle and
the realistic applicability.
We plan to submerge the test structure in a bath mixture of PVA + PVP + water
+ borax (mix). The borax within the solution should increase the degradation
time of the PVA and we believe that the coating won’t degrade until the end of
the experiment, but further analysis is needed. We would most likely apply the
coating as close to launch as possible.
Further investigation indicates that the coating experiences degradation over
time. This is because the PVA + PVP + water + borax mixture contains water,
which bonds quite well with other water molecules that are inside our test chamber. When full degradation occurs, the hydrophilic coating is lost, but even if this
fully occurs, we don’t expect it to significantly affect our experiment because of
the nature of the base material being hydrophilic on its own (the contact angle
of SLA printed plastic is still low, < 50°).
From an experiment where the coating sample was fully submerged in water, it
was observed that full degradation occurred within ~12 hours. Realistically this
time would be a lot longer in a real experiment environment due to the limited
liquid amount.

4.5.3 CFD Overview
Large part of the CAPILUX experiment is based on verifying the predicted fluid
behaviour in microgravity. To have some predictions to verify, a model must be
created which then can be analysed, both analytically and in form of simulations.
Where applicable analytical equations will be utilized but due to the dynamic nature of fluid distribution CFD simulations must also be made. To do this ANSYS
Fluent is employed.
In ANSYS the computational domain is made as a cylinder identical in shape
to the test chamber inner cylinder (46mm diameter, 72mm length). Simulation
subjects (slosh baffle, test shapes, etc.) are subtracted from the computational
domain.
Meshing is done in such a way to ensure that areas of higher interest (test
shapes, corners, small structures) are refined with sufficient resolution.
Because the test chambers are offset from the rockets spin axis rotating reference frame with offset must be utilized. Transient CFD simulations are ran. To
more realistically simulate the fluid distribution, REXUS 33 flight data (acceleration, angular rate) is used and imported into ANSYS as a value profile.
ANSYS Simulation and solver settings are written in and if not further indicated,

4.6 Electronics Design
4.6.1 Overview
The mainboards box is mounted to the bulkhead with 8 M3 standoffs. Camera
DVRs are mounted next to each of the cameras to the bulkhead. The mainboards functions are as follows:
• Voltage filtering and conversion to +5V and +3.3V,
• Receiving SOE; LO signals from RXSM,
• Controlling cameras and LEDs,
• Measuring onboard acceleration and environmental data,
• Storing data to flash and an SD card,
• Communicating with ground station via RXSM,
• Interfacing with chamber sensors,
• Controlling the TV video channel multiplexer (mux) board.
The mainboard is connected via a D-SUB connector to the RXSM, JST SH series
connectors are used for connecting to the cameras, chamber sensors, chamber
LEDs and TV channel mux board. Chamber lights and sensors are mounted on
a small PCB that is then integrated into the chamber caps. For each chamber
one cap only contains LEDs while the other side contains both LEDs and the
pressure sensors. The circuit diagrams and PCB layouts can be viewed in the
Appendix C.
After the comments made in CDR, the camera, TV Mux, and chamber connectors were changed from the unreliable JST SH to Molex Micro-Fit 3.0 series
connectors, which are rated for much higher current (up to 5A per pin vs. 1A for
JST SH) and offer a more robust, positive-locking mating interface with a higher
rated number of mating cycles.
Per Molex’s Micro-Fit 3.0 product specification (PS-43045-001, §4.6), mating
durability is rated for 30 cycles (EIA-364-1000), with contact resistance required
to stay within 20 mΩ of its initial value. A controlled use plan keeps actual use
well within this budget: approximately 8 cycles for prototyping and bench functional testing, and 2 cycles at each subsequent integration milestone (mechanical integration, vibration testing, and final pre-campaign integration), for an estimated total of 14 cycles – less than half the rated durability, leaving comfortable
margin for contingency.
After CDR the mainboard’s PCB DSUB connector was flipped to the other side
and changed to be perpendicular to the PCB (aligned with the wire harness
direction).

experiment chamber during flight. We also request video transmission throughout the flight as a redundancy measure, ensuring that some footage remains
accessible in the event that SD cards cannot be recovered.

4.8 Power System
4.8.1 Power regulation
The experiment receives an unregulated +28 V supply bus provided by the
REXUS service module via the experiment connector, while all on-board digital
electronics require stable low-voltage rails (5V and 3.3V). A direct linear conversion from 28V to low voltage rails would cause excessive dissipation and is
therefore avoided. Instead, the experiment uses a staged conversion approach
from 28 V converting to 5 V using a high-efficiency DC/DC buck converter and
from 5 V converting to 3.3 V using an LDO (low dropout) regulator.
Local decoupling capacitors are placed at the input and output of each regulator to ensure stability and minimize ripple. High-frequency decoupling is also
provided for sensors.

4.9 Software Design
4.9.1 Software concept and objectives
The experiment software has four responsibilities: (1) execute the mission timeline deterministically, (2) acquire and store experiment data with timestamps,
(3) provide a command and telemetry interface for pre-flight checks and inflight monitoring, and (4) manage onboard memory safely, including protection
against accidental data loss.
The experiment must run correctly whether or not the ground link is available,
because the uplink is turned off at lift-off and the downlink has a possibility dropping out during flight. The software is therefore autonomous once started and
never waits on a ground command to progress the timeline. It must be fail-safe,
because the recorded data is the primary experiment product. The logger and
memory handling are built around never losing already written data. And it must
be deterministic, because the experiment window is short and fixed, so state
changes are driven by well-defined triggers. On power-up the system boots,
arms a hardware watchdog, establishes communication with the ground station
and enters a standby state. A dedicated test mode allows all subsystems (cameras, LEDs, sensors, data logging and the communication link) to be exercised
from the ground station without initiating the experiment sequence. The experiment sequence is triggered exclusively by the SOE signal.
The main software objectives are:
• deterministic mission state progression driven by SOE and elapsed time,
• time-stamped acquisition and storage of all sensor and housekeeping data,
• a controllable pre-flight test mode, including camera and LED activation for a
visual GO/NO-GO confirmation before launch,
• continuous downlink of experiment state and housekeeping telemetry,
• protected memory-management commands (two-step confirmation for destructive operations),
• preservation of recorded data in the event of power interruption,
• automatic recovery from software lock-up through the hardware watchdog.
4.9.2 Software architecture
Implementation framework. The flight computer software is implemented in
C++ using the Arduino framework under PlatformIO, targeting the RP2040 microcontroller. This framework was selected because it provides a stable, well
supported environment for RP2040 class devices while allowing direct registerlevel access to the GPIO, SPI, I2C, UART amd PWM peripherals used by the
experiment.
Execution model. The software runs as a single cooperative main loop with
a fixed scheduling pattern, rather than a preemptive multitasking system. Each
pass of the loop feeds the watchdog, services any received command, and then
runs the periodic work (sensor sampling, telemetry, logging) whose scheduled
time has elapsed. A cooperative loop was chosen over an RTOS because the
RX38_CAPILUX_SED_v3-0_26Aug26

- 108 -
workload is light and periodi. A single-threaded design removes the need for
locking between tasks and makes the timing behaviour easy to reason about
and to verify. Because every activity is short, the loop period stays well below
the watchdog timeout under all normal conditions.
Structure. The onboard software is organised as functional modules around
a central mission state machine. Each module owns one domain and can be
developed in isolation on the flight computer PCB before full integration:
• State machine. Holds the current mission state (Section 4.9.6) and performs
all transitions based on the SOE input, elapsed time, and ground commands.
It is the single point that decides what the experiment is doing at any moment,
so that no other module has to reason about mission phase. The other modules simply act according to the current state.
• Command parser. Reads newline terminated commands from the communication interface, splits each into a command word and optional argument, and
dispatches it. Unknown or malformed commands are rejected, counted in a
bad-command counter, and reported with an error packet. The parser is written so that partial or corrupted input can never advance the mission state or
trigger a destructive action.
• Telemetry generator. Assembles the downlink packets defined in Table 4.27
and Table 4.28 and writes them to the communication interface. Packets are
produced on a fixed schedule or on request in response to a command.
• Acquisition manager. Samples the four chamber pressure/temperature sensors, the onboard barometer and the IMU at 5 Hz. Each reading is validity
checked. A sensor that does not respond is marked invalid and its values are
reported as null rather than as a substituted number, and it is retried on the
next cycle so that a sensor which returns is picked up automatically without a
restart.
• Data logger. Appends one time tagged record per second to a CSV file on the
SD card (Section 4.9.7), containing the mission state, the input and actuator
states, the command counters, and every sensor value. It also manages file
creation, the save marker, and safe closure of the file on command and at the
end of the experiment.
• Memory manager. Implements the SAVE_MEMORY operation, which finalises
and flushes the current log, and the protected two-step CLEAR_MEMORY operation (Section 4.9.5). It also reports memory state (mount status, logger
health, file size and line count) in the memory telemetry packet.
• Observation control. Switches camera power through the camera enable line
and drives the four chamber LED channels by PWM, both on ground command
in test mode and automatically during the flight sequence.
• Diagnostics. Feeds the hardware watchdog every loop, and maintains the
health information reported in housekeeping: overall status, per-sensor validity, sensor count, SD-card mount state, and the command counters.
Watchdog. The RP2040 hardware watchdog is armed at boot with a 4 s timeout
and is fed once per main loop iteration. The timeout is set to several times the
RX38_CAPILUX_SED_v3-0_26Aug26

- 109 -
nominal loop period, so that ordinary work never trips it, while still detecting a
genuine lock-up within a few seconds. The watchdog remains armed during all
mission states.

4.9.5 Memory protection
To protect against accidental wiping of recorded data, destructive memory operations use a two step confirmation. A single command cannot erase data,
which prevents an accidental key press or a false command from destroying a
recording:
1. CLEAR_MEMORY arms the operation and downlinks a CFR (confirmationrequired) packet. Nothing is erased.
2. CLEAR_MEMORY CONFIRM, received within a 5 s window, performs the erase
and is acknowledged. Outside the window the arm state expires automatically and the sequence must be restarted, so a stale confirmation cannot
take effect later.
In addition, CLEAR_MEMORY is inhibited entirely once the flight sequence has
started, so recorded flight data cannot be cleared in flight regardless of confirmation. The ground station GUI exposes the two steps as two separate buttons,
both marked as protected commands.
4.9.6 Mission states and control logic
The software is organised around a small set of mission states. Keeping the
number of states low makes the flight behaviour easy to verify and leaves little
room for unintended transitions.
Boot. Entered on power-up. The watchdog is armed, the interfaces, sensors
and SD logger are initialised, and a power-on packet is transmitted. Once initialisation completes the software moves to Standby.
Standby. The default pre-flight state and the state re-entered after a watchdog
reboot. Communication with the ground station is established, and the software transmits the state and housekeeping telemetry at 5 Hz and writes one
log record per second. The experiment is idle: cameras and LEDs are off and
the flight sequence has not started. From Standby the operator can enter Test
Mode, or the software will begin the flight sequence on a valid SOE.
Test Mode. Entered on ground command (T−6 min in the countdown). All subsystems can be exercised without starting the flight experiment: sensors are
read and reported, LEDs and cameras can be powered, and memory commands
can be run. Cameras and LEDs are switched on during this window and their
status is downlinked, so the team can confirm full experiment readiness before
RX38_CAPILUX_SED_v3-0_26Aug26

- 114 -
the final launch GO. Test Mode cannot start the flight sequence and is left with
an explicit command back to Standby.
Experiment Active. Entered from Standby on a valid SOE signal (around
T−0:45 min). Camera and LED power is enabled, recording starts, and sensor and housekeeping records are written continuously with timestamps. The
state progresses autonomously and does not depend on the ground link.
Safe Shutdown. Entered when the experiment window ends. Recording stops,
the data logger finalises and closes all open files, and cameras and LEDs are
powered off. Only status telemetry continues until payload power is removed.
Safe Mode. A fallback state that can be entered on command or on an internal
fault before flight. Cameras and LEDs are disabled while telemetry continues,
and the state is left by command or by restarting. It provides a defined, harmless
condition to fall back to rather than continuing in an unknown state.
Role of the LO signal. The LO (lift-off) signal is not used as a control trigger.
The flight sequence is fully defined by SOE and elapsed time, which removes
any dependence on LO detection reliability. LO is monitored and included in the
STA telemetry packet and the onboard log purely as a recorded mission event,
providing a timestamp reference that correlates onboard data with the actual liftoff during post-flight analysis. It has therefore been removed from the software
control flow chart and appears only in the data record.

4.9.7 Data logging format
All acquired data is written to a single CSV file on the SD card, one record per
second, so that the complete experiment history can be reconstructed and crosschecked against the downlinked telemetry after flight. A plain CSV format was
chosen because it is human readable, trivially importable into any analysis tool,
and robust: a truncated file caused by an unexpected power loss still yields all
records written up to that point.
Each record contains, in order: the log line number, the millisecond timestamp,
the mission state, the SOE and LO input states, the camera power state, the
four LED PWM values, the received-command and bad-command counters,
and then the sensor block: chamber 1–4 pressure and temperature, onboard
barometer pressure and temperature, IMU acceleration on three axes, and IMU
angular rate on three axes. A missing sensor value is written as an empty field,
mirroring the null convention used in telemetry, so that gaps are distinguishable from real readings during analysis. The column header is written when the
file is created, so the file is self-describing.
RX38_CAPILUX_SED_v3-0_26Aug26

- 117 -
When SAVE_MEMORY is executed, the logger flushes and finalises the file and
writes a small separate save marker file recording the timestamp, the log file
path and the log size at the moment of saving. This provides an explicit record
that a save was performed and makes it easy to confirm on the ground that data
has been committed to the card.
4.9.8 Principal testing with electronics
Software verification follows three levels of increasing realism, from logic-only
checks to a full end-to-end rehearsal on representative hardware. The intent is
to catch behavioural errors early, in the simplest environment that can expose
them, before repeating the check with more of the real system present.
Level 1 – Logic verification (software only). The state machine is exercised
with simulated SOE events and synthetic sensor streams to verify mode transitions, the recording stop criterion and data record formatting, independently of
hardware.
Level 2 – Electronics in the loop (bench prototype). The real flight computer
PCB is used with the actual power path, real SOE and LO inputs, real sensors,
an actual SD card, and the wired ground interface, to validate behaviour under
power cycles, correct trigger detection, stable sampling timing and data integrity.
Level 3 – End-to-end rehearsal. A full scripted timeline (power-on to SOE to
experiment to shutdown) is executed, all data is recorded with no lost information, and a post-run comparison of expected against recorded event markers
and timing is performed.
The detailed software verification activities, several of which use the RXSM Simulator now available to the team, are defined in the Ground Support Equipment
section (Section 4.10) and referenced from the verification matrix in Chapter 5.

Ground station software framework
The ground station prototype consists of a Svelte graphical user interface and
a Node.js communication bridge. The Svelte GUI runs in a browser and is responsible for displaying experiment status, chamber status, housekeeping data,
command buttons, mission timeline information and raw telemetry. The GUI
does not access the serial port directly.
The Node.js bridge runs on the ground station computer and connects the
browser-based GUI to the flight computer. On one side, it opens the flight computer serial connection. On the other side, it provides a WebSocket interface
for the GUI. Telemetry received from the flight computer is forwarded to the GUI,
and commands sent from the GUI are forwarded to the flight computer. In the
final ground setup, the ground station computer will be connected through the
mission provided ground interface using an Ethernet cable.
To realize the ground support, the team designs a custom graphical user interface which shall support the following objectives:
1. Transmitting: The ground station shall send commands to the flight computer before lift-off. During this time, tests are run and the signals for them
are transmitted from the ground station.
2. Receiving: During the pre-launch tests (and also during flight and experiment), the status of the sensors and system is sent to the ground station
and shown on the GUI. There are SD cards onboard the module on which
camera data will already be saved, and video will be transmitted through the
TV channel. Since the visual feedback from the cameras is already shown
there, no camera-status update needs to be sent. Data from the IMU and
barometers is sent to the ground station in case it is impossible to recover
data afterwards. This also ensures that a chamber failure, for example a leak
or another cause that ruins the experiment in a specific chamber, is known
immediately.
3. Visualization: Any received data is displayed on the GUI for the team members to observe the experiment’s progress and status. The GUI is organized
hierarchically, with the main board at the top showing its barometer and IMU
data (acceleration, rotation, orientation) in clear numeric and simple visual
formats. Below, each experimental chamber has its own panel displaying its
pressure and temperature readings in a consistent layout. Colour indicators
are used for status and small trend graphs for changes over time, keeping
the interface clear and easy to compare across chambers.Design of the GUI
is shown in Figure 4.40.

Fault detection and handling
The software is designed to detect basic faults and to degrade gracefully rather
than stop. If communication with the ground station is lost, the experiment continues autonomous operation and resumes telemetry when the link is restored.
Invalid or corrupted commands are ignored, counted, and reported on the GUI
and in telemetry. If a sensor returns no data or invalid data, it is marked as
faulty while the software continues operation with the remaining sensors. If the
SD card or flash memory becomes full or a write operation fails, data writing
stops safely and the condition is reported, while video transfer to the TV channel is unaffected. If the software reaches an invalid internal state before flight,
it switches to a safe fallback mode that can be exited by a dedicated GUI command.
RXSM Simulator and ground test setup
For ground testing and pre-flight checkout, the team uses the SSC REXUS Service Module Simulator (RX variant) as ground support equipment. The simulator
reproduces the electrical interface of the rocket service module on the bench:
it supplies the experiment with 28 V power, provides the SOE, LO and SODS
control-interface signals through push buttons, and exposes the bidirectional
RS-422 ground link at 38.4 kbit/s. This allows the complete switch on and countdown sequence to be rehearsed without the real service module. During bench
development the identical software and packet structure is also exercised over
USB CDC, so that the software can be verified before the RS-422 hardware is
available.
The simulator additionally provides a link-error generator, which can inject
single-bit errors and communication dropouts into the serial stream. This is
used to verify that the flight software rejects corrupted commands and that the
telemetry link resynchronises after a dropout, without any incorrect command
being executed.
